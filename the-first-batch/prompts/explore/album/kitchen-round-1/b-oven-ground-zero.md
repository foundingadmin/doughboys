A real 1970s American home magazine interior photograph shot on medium format film with an 80mm lens, fine film grain, muted warm print color like a 1974 Kodachrome page.

Ground zero of a baking explosion in a 1970s American home kitchen. A harvest gold freestanding range with analog oven dials and a small glowing red pilot light, its oven door blown wide open and bent. Out of the oven a huge cohesive mass of raw bread dough has heaved and slumped down the open door onto the floor in one thick wide fold, as wide as the oven, never a thin rope, never coiled, never arched like an arm. Around the range: dark walnut laminate cabinets with doors blown off their hinges, one hanging crooked by a single hinge, a drawer half torn out spilling wooden spoons and a rolling pin. Avocado green countertop with chrome trim, a dented aluminum canister set tipped over, a stack of nesting glass mixing bowls in nine colors, blueberry blue, butter yellow, cherry red, bondi teal, bubblegum pink, lime, grape purple, tangerine and mint, scattered and one cracked. Thick white flour coats everything like fresh snow and drifts against the baseboards on an orange and brown geometric linoleum floor, a fine haze of flour still hanging in the air.

Raw dough: firm and moist, freshly mixed raw bread dough, thick, heavy, plump and saggy, one cohesive mass in one even pale oatmeal beige like raw pizza dough, never pink and never meaty. Satin matte skin with fine stretch lines and soft folds. Never dry or stringy: no strands, threads or torn edges, no holes, no lace, no webbing. Where it meets the floor it stays thick and rounded. Shaped only by gravity, an object and never a creature. Nothing pokes out like a tongue, no accidental faces.

Camera straight on at the oven's own height, squared up to the front of the range, the range centered and filling about half the frame height, the broken cabinets framing it. Square frame. Soft daylight from the upper left.

No people, no hands, no logos, no brand names, no readable text.
